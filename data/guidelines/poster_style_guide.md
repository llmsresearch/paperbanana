# Poster Design Guide

Grounded in conference-published design advice (CVPR poster guidance,
ICML/NeurIPS conventions), the #betterposter movement's findings, and
analysis of award-winning AI-conference posters. A poster is an
advertisement for a 3-minute conversation, not a compressed paper.

## The five-second rule

A passerby decides in ~5 seconds whether to stop. The poster must answer
three questions at a glance: What problem? What's the one-sentence
takeaway? What's the headline number?

- Lead with a **takeaway statement**, not background. The single most
  important sentence of the paper belongs near the top, visually
  dominant, phrased as a finding ("X achieves Y at Z cost"), never as a
  topic ("A study of X").
- The **headline result** (the number reviewers cited) appears twice:
  once as a large standalone claim, once in its results figure/table.

## Content budget

- 600-800 words total across the whole poster. Award-winning posters
  average far less text than first drafts; cut until it hurts.
- Bullets, never paragraphs. One line per bullet. Each bullet earns its
  place by answering a question a visitor would actually ask.
- Figures should carry ~40-50% of the visual area. When in doubt, grow
  the figure and cut a bullet.
- Methods get less space than authors want: one overview figure plus
  3-4 bullets. Visitors who care will ask; the paper has the details.

## Narrative order

1. Motivation/problem (short, punchy)
2. Takeaway + method overview (the visual centerpiece)
3. Results (headline figure/table with the number called out)
4. Analysis/ablations (only the surprising ones)
5. Conclusion + QR code to paper/code

Reading flow is top-to-bottom within a column, columns left-to-right.
Never make a visitor's eye jump backward.

## Visual hierarchy

- Exactly one accent color, used only for emphasis (headline numbers,
  key arrows, the takeaway). Everything else stays quiet.
- Panel titles are questions or claims when possible ("Why does early
  boosting work?" beats "Analysis").
- Whitespace is structure, not waste — but it belongs BETWEEN groups,
  not pooled inside half-empty boxes. Dense, balanced panels with clear
  gutters read as professional; stretched sparse panels read as unfinished.
- Tables are the weakest poster element: prefer a chart of the key
  comparison; if a table must stay, bold the winning row/cell and cut
  every column a visitor won't ask about.

## Things that mark a poster as amateur

- Walls of text, full sentences from the paper's abstract
- More than ~8 panels, or panels under 3 lines of content
- Figures with axis labels smaller than body text
- Mixed font families beyond heading + body
- No QR code / no way to find the paper afterward
- The conclusion restating the abstract instead of saying what to do
  with the finding
